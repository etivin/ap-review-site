Unit Overview

The Age of Total War

Between 1900 and 1945, the world experienced two catastrophic global wars that together killed over 70 million people. The causes were deeply rooted in the preceding century: imperial rivalries, hyper-nationalism, arms races, and the rigid alliance systems of European great powers. When Archduke Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo in June 1914, these pressures detonated into the most destructive war the world had ever seen.

WWI shattered the old European order. The Ottoman, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and German empires all collapsed. The harsh terms of the Versailles Treaty, combined with the global Great Depression of the 1930s, created the economic and psychological conditions in which fascism could flourish. Hitler's rise in Germany, Mussolini's in Italy, and militarism in Japan produced leaders willing to gamble civilization itself on conquest.

World War II was even more total than the first, civilian populations were deliberate targets. The Holocaust, the Japanese war in China, and Allied strategic bombing campaigns killed millions of non-combatants. The war ended with atomic weapons deployed against Japanese cities, inaugurating the nuclear age and setting the stage for the Cold War that would define the second half of the 20th century.

Core Themes
01
Causes of WWI (MAIN)
Militarism, Alliance systems, Imperialism, and Nationalism created a powder keg by 1914. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand provided the spark that ignited a continent-wide war within six weeks.
02
Total War & New Technology
Industrial warfare introduced machine guns, poison gas, tanks, submarines, and airplanes. Both world wars mobilized entire economies and societies, blurring the line between soldiers and civilians.
03
Interwar Instability
The Great Depression devastated economies worldwide, discrediting liberal democracy in many nations. The result was the rise of fascism in Germany and Italy, and military nationalism in Japan.
04
Mass Atrocities
The Holocaust systematically murdered 6 million Jews and 5 million others. The Armenian Genocide, Japanese war crimes in Nanjing, and Soviet gulags represent deliberate state-directed mass murder on an unprecedented scale.
05
Anti-Colonial Nationalism
WWI exposed the contradictions of European claims to "civilization" and democracy. Colonial subjects who fought for empire returned home demanding self-determination, planting seeds of later independence movements.
06
Propaganda & Mobilization
Both wars required unprecedented mass mobilization. Governments deployed propaganda through posters, film, and radio to sustain public support for industrialized slaughter on a scale never before seen.

2024 AP Exam: Short Answer Question 2 (Primary Source)
Source: 1932 German Election Poster

National Socialist (Nazi) Party election poster, Germany, 1932. Text reads: “Men! Women! Millions of men without work. Millions of children without a future. Save the German family. Vote Adolf Hitler!”

A. Identify ONE likely political purpose of this image. (1 point)
B. Explain ONE way the image illustrates the economic situation of the period after the First World War. (1 point)
C. Explain ONE way the rise of the German National Socialist Party led to the Second World War. (1 point)
SAQ Tips from Mr. T Label each part (A, B, C). Identify = 1 sentence. Explain = 2–3 sentences with “because.” No full paragraphs: answer only what is asked.
Show model answer

A. The poster’s political purpose was to win votes for Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party in the 1932 election by blaming the Weimar government for Germany’s unemployment.

B. The poster shows a struggling family and says that millions of German men were out of work, which reflects the mass unemployment of the Great Depression. Germany’s economy had already been weakened by the reparations required by the Treaty of Versailles, so when American loans dried up after the 1929 crash, unemployment soared to around six million by 1932.

C. After taking power in 1933, the Nazis rearmed Germany in violation of the Treaty of Versailles because Hitler wanted to overturn the treaty and win “living space” in Eastern Europe. This expansion led Germany to annex Austria and the Sudetenland in 1938 and then invade Poland in 1939, which caused Britain and France to declare war.

2024 AP Exam: Document-Based Question 1

Evaluate the extent to which economic motives were the leading cause of Japanese imperialism in the period circa 1900–1945.

DBQ Formula from Mr. T ¶1: Thesis + Contextualization (pre-1900 Japan). ¶2–3: Group documents by theme (economic vs. non-economic motives). Apply HIPP to 2 docs. Include 1 piece of outside evidence not in the documents. Aim for 4+ documents used.
Show model answer

In the decades surrounding this topic, industrialized Western powers built vast empires in Asia and Africa, and Japan, after the Meiji Restoration of 1868, industrialized rapidly so that it would not be colonized itself. Japan copied Western models of industry, military organization, and empire, defeating China in 1895 and taking Taiwan. Because Japan was a small island nation with few natural resources, its growing factories depended on imported coal, iron, and cotton. This wider situation is the backdrop against which Japanese imperialism from 1900 to 1945 should be understood. Economic motives were largely the leading cause of Japanese imperialism, as seen through Japan’s need for raw materials for its industries and the business community’s push to protect its trade in Manchuria.

One way economic motives drove Japanese imperialism was through the need for raw materials, as seen in Documents 2, 5, and 6, which show Japan’s lack of resources and its dependence on imports. Document 5 is a 1937 New York Times article explaining that Japan’s offensive in northern China aimed to control the iron ore and coal of Shaanxi province, which Japan’s steel industry desperately needed. This document supports my argument because it demonstrates that Japan’s war in China was tied directly to resources for its industries. The author of Document 5 is an American journalist writing for American readers. Therefore, he may emphasize Japan’s hunger for raw materials, which means he presents the invasion mainly as a resource grab. This supports my argument because even an outside observer saw economics at the heart of Japanese expansion. Document 1 is a 1903 editorial in Japan’s leading business journal arguing that Japanese businessmen wanted war with Russia because Russian port taxes threatened to cut Japan out of the Manchurian trade. This document supports my argument because it demonstrates that business interests pushed for expansion decades before the 1930s.

Strategic and nationalist motives also mattered, as seen in Documents 3, 4, and 7, which show Japan seeking security against Russia and spreading its culture in conquered lands. Document 4 is a 1931 essay by Kanji Ishiwara arguing that Japan needed northern Manchuria to block Russia and stabilize its control over Korea. The author of Document 4 is a Japanese army officer stationed in Manchuria. Therefore, he may emphasize military security over trade, which means his essay shows how the army justified expansion as national defense. This qualifies my argument because military leaders, not just businessmen, pushed for empire. Document 7, a photograph of a Japanese language class in occupied Singapore published in a major Japanese newspaper, also shows Japan presenting itself as the leader of Asia while imposing its language.

An additional piece of evidence that supports my argument that economic motives were the leading cause is the U.S. oil embargo of 1941, which cut off most of Japan’s oil and led Japan to plan the conquest of the oil-rich Dutch East Indies. This evidence supports my argument because it demonstrates how Japan’s need for raw materials drove its expansion into Southeast Asia and its attack on Pearl Harbor.

Economic and strategic motives were intertwined, and even some Japanese experts doubted that empire paid. Konoe (Doc 2) blended economic fears with resentment of British and American power, while the economist Toichi Nawa (Doc 6) warned that empire building made Japan more dependent on imported raw materials, not less. This shows that economic motives drove imperialism largely because leaders believed empire would bring security, even when the economics did not add up.

2019 AP Exam: Long Essay Question 4

“In the period after 1900, the role of the state in the economy varied, with many states adopting policies to control or manage their economies.

Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which one or more states controlled their economies in this time period.”

LEQ Formula from Mr. T No documents: all from memory. ¶1: Thesis (evaluative adverb + line of reasoning) + Contextualization (pre-1900 context). ¶2: Evidence 1: describe AND explain how it supports your argument. ¶3: Evidence 2. Frame around causation, CCOT, or comparison.
Show model answer

Before 1900, most industrialized countries followed laissez-faire ideas, associated with Adam Smith, that governments should stay out of the economy. Industrialization created great wealth but also harsh working conditions and inequality, which led Karl Marx and other socialists to argue that workers or the state should control production. Some states, such as Meiji Japan, had already used government support to build industry quickly. This set the stage for the twentieth century because governments now had both a reason and a model for taking a larger role in their economies. Many states largely controlled their economies in this period, as seen through the Soviet Union’s Five-Year Plans and China’s Great Leap Forward.

Stalin’s Five-Year Plans support my argument that many states largely controlled their economies because they demonstrate how, beginning in 1928, the Soviet government set production targets for every factory, collectivized farms, and poured resources into heavy industry such as steel and coal. Because the state controlled all investment, the Soviet Union industrialized rapidly, but forced collectivization led to a famine that killed millions, especially in Ukraine.

Mao’s Great Leap Forward supports my argument because it demonstrates how, beginning in 1958, the Chinese Communist Party organized peasants into huge communes and ordered them to produce steel in backyard furnaces. Because the state controlled planting and labor, local officials reported false harvests to meet their targets, which resulted in a famine that killed tens of millions of people.

State control varied in degree and changed over time. In response to the Great Depression, democracies also expanded state control without abolishing private property: Franklin Roosevelt’s New Deal created public works and regulated banks, and after 1945 Britain nationalized coal, railways, and steel and built a welfare state. Later, many states reduced their control, as China under Deng Xiaoping introduced market reforms after 1978, which shows that state control of economies rose and then fell across the twentieth century.

Topic-by-Topic Analysis
7.1: Shifting Power After 1900 ▼

Three land empires collapse in the same generation: the Qing (1911), the Russian (1917), and the Ottoman (partitioned 1923). Japan and the U.S. emerge as new global powers, challenging European dominance.

Key argument: Japan's rise exposed the limits of Western racial hierarchy, the West refused Japan equal status at Versailles, fueling the very imperialism they claimed to oppose.

Meiji RestorationQing CollapseRusso-Japanese WarRacial Equality Proposal
7.2: Causes of WWI (MAIN) ▼

Long-term tensions (MAIN) made Europe explosive; the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand was the spark, not the cause. The alliance system turned a regional crisis into a world war in weeks.

Key argument: The Treaty of Versailles (1919) ended WWI but planted the conditions for WWII, the War Guilt Clause, reparations, and territorial losses created a crisis of legitimacy in Germany.

MAINTriple EntenteFranz FerdinandTreaty of VersaillesWar Guilt Clause
7.3: WWI & Total War ▼

Total war mobilized entire economies and societies. Machine guns and barbed wire produced the Western Front stalemate. Governments used propaganda, censorship, and rationing to sustain civilian commitment over years of devastating conflict.

Key argument: Total war blurred the soldier/civilian line, the home front became as strategically important as the battlefield. This redefined what states could demand from their populations.

Total WarTrench WarfarePoison GasPropagandaRussian Revolution
7.4: The Interwar Economy ▼

The Great Depression (1929) collapsed world trade by 50% and devastated democratic governments. Responses varied sharply: Keynesian spending (U.S. New Deal), centralized planning (Soviet Five Year Plans), and import substitution (Latin America).

Key argument: Economic desperation made populations willing to accept radical solutions, the Depression was the key enabling condition for fascist movements in Europe.

Great DepressionNew DealFive Year PlansImport SubstitutionPemex
7.5: Resistance & Nationalism ▼

WWI weakened European empires and raised expectations of self-determination, promises that were not kept. Resistance movements intensified, from Gandhi's nonviolent civil disobedience to armed anti-colonial uprisings.

Key argument: European states used the language of democracy and self-determination to justify war, but denied those same rights to colonized peoples, creating a legitimacy crisis that accelerated decolonization after WWII.

Salt MarchSelf-DeterminationNégritudePan-Africanism
7.6: Appeasement & WWII ▼

The slides walk through four key appeasement moments: Japan’s invasion of Manchuria (League fails to act), Hitler’s Rhineland remilitarization, the annexation of Austria, and finally the Munich Agreement, where Chamberlain handed Hitler the Sudetenland and declared “Peace for our time.”

Pattern to see: Each time the Allies failed to act, Hitler grew bolder. Japan observed the same weakness and escalated in China. This is the lesson of appeasement: rewarding aggression signals that more aggression is safe.

AP angle: Appeasement is a Complexity move, argue that WWI exhaustion explains Allied inaction, but that inaction made WWII inevitable. Don’t just say “appeasement was bad.” Explain why rational leaders made that choice and why it still failed.

AppeasementMunich AgreementRhinelandSudetenlandLeague of NationsChamberlain
7.7: Genocide & Mass Atrocities ▼

The slides cover three genocide case studies: the Armenian Genocide (Ottoman Empire, 1915–1923), the Cambodian Genocide (Khmer Rouge, 1975–1979), and the Rwandan Genocide (1994). The Holocaust and the Nanjing Massacre (“Rape of Nanking”) are also central to Unit 7.

Key argument: Modern state capacity: bureaucracy, railway networks, propaganda, industrial technology, made 20th-century genocides possible at a scale impossible in pre-industrial societies. The state did not just permit these killings; the state organized them.

AP angle: Use genocide as evidence for the broader argument that total war and authoritarian nationalism dehumanized out-groups. The Nanjing Massacre connects directly to Japanese imperial expansion; the Armenian Genocide connects to Ottoman collapse and WWI.

HolocaustArmenian GenocideNanjing MassacreRwandan GenocideCambodian GenocideGenocide
7.8: Nationalism & Mass Communication ▼

Radio, film, and print allowed states to mobilize entire populations. Propaganda shaped what civilians understood about the war. States suppressed bad news (Britain never announced the sinking of HMS Audacious) while amplifying narratives of national sacrifice.

Key argument: The development of mass media created a new form of state power, the ability to shape popular belief at scale was as important to total war as industrial production.
