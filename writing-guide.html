Writing Skills
The Free-Response Section

The essays are a game with published rules.

You are not being asked to write a beautiful essay. You are being asked to earn specific points off a checklist a reader is holding. Once you know exactly what each point requires, the essays stop being mysterious. This guide walks you through all three writing tasks (the SAQ, the LEQ, and the DBQ): what each one is, how to build it, and how to win every point on the rubric.

“A grader spends about two minutes on your essay. Your job is to make every point obvious, in order, so they never have to hunt for it.”

01.  The landscape: three essays, one exam

The free-response half of the AP exam has three writing tasks. They test the same historical thinking, but they ask for it in very different sizes. Know what you are walking into before you learn the moves.

SAQ Short-Answer LEQ Long Essay DBQ Document-Based
What it is 3 short questions, each with parts a, b, c, all required. Bullet-style answers, no essay. One required essay from a single broad prompt (the 2027 exam no longer gives a choice of three). No documents; you supply the evidence. One essay built from 7 provided documents plus your own knowledge.
Points 3 points (1 per part) 6 points 7 points
Share of exam 20% 15% 25%
Time ~40 min for all 3 (about 12–13 min each) ~40 min ~60 min (includes a 15-min reading period)
Thesis? No. Just answer the task. Yes, required and scored Yes, required and scored
The one-line goal Answer, Cite, Explain. Fast, specific, done. Build and defend an argument with your own evidence, framed by a reasoning skill. Build an argument using the documents and outside knowledge, and analyze the sources.
Read the weighting againThe three writing tasks together are 60% of your entire exam score, more than the multiple choice. This is where the exam is won or lost.
Built for the 2027 exam redesignCollege Board updated the AP History free-response section starting with the 2027 exam. The two biggest changes: you now answer all three SAQs (no more skipping one), and the LEQ is a single required prompt instead of a choice of three. The point thresholds below reflect the updated rubrics your class uses.
The Master Key
02.  The words that tell you what to do

Every prompt is built from a small set of command verbs and reasoning skills. Most lost points come from doing the wrong task: describing when you were told to explain, listing when you were told to argue. Learn these once and every prompt on every exam becomes readable.

A. Command verbs: the size of the answer
One sentence

Identify

State a correct fact. No explanation needed. Just name the thing the prompt asks for.

“Identify one cause of…”

1 to 2 sentences

Describe

Give the specific features or details of a thing. More than a name, but no cause-and-effect required.

“Describe one similarity between…”

2 to 3 sentences

Explain

Show how or why, connecting a cause to an effect with a mechanism. This is where most SAQ points are lost.

“Explain one reason why…”

A whole essay

Evaluate / Explain how

The essay prompts ask you to build and defend an argument. A DBQ usually says “evaluate the extent to which” (take a position on how much); a 2027 LEQ may instead say “explain how.” Either way, argue a claim, do not just describe.

“Evaluate the extent to which…” · “Explain how…”

The tellIf you see “Evaluate the extent to which,” the reader wants a judgment with a qualifier: largely, primarily, to a limited extent, more than. A flat answer with no degree of judgment cannot earn the thesis point.
B. Reasoning skills: the shape of the argument

An LEQ or DBQ is built on a reasoning skill that structures the whole thing. Pick one and build your body paragraphs around it.

Cause and effect

Causation

Argues what led to what, and why. Organize around causes and their consequences.

Because X happened, Y followed…

Then vs. now

Continuity & Change

Argues what stayed the same and what shifted across the period. Organize around before and after.

Early on X; by the end this had become Y…

A vs. B

Comparison

Argues similarities and differences between regions, groups, or eras, and why they differ.

Whereas A did X, B did Y, revealing…

Task One
03.  The SAQ: Short-Answer Question
The Warm-Up That Counts

Answer, Cite, Explain

3 parts (a, b, c)
3 points total
~13 min each
No thesis needed

The SAQ is the most winnable task on the exam and the fastest to master. There is no thesis, no introduction, no conclusion, so do not write any. You answer parts a, b, and c directly, in complete sentences, and move on. Label your answers (a) (b) (c) so the reader can find each point instantly.

The three SAQs (2027)You answer all three now, and each is anchored to a different kind of stimulus and a different era between 1200 and 2001: SAQ 1 uses a secondary source (a historian's argument), SAQ 2 uses a primary source, and SAQ 3 uses a non-text source (a map, chart, image, or cartoon). Some parts are pure reading comprehension; others ask you to bring in your own knowledge or to source the author's point of view.
The A.C.E. move for every part
  1. Answer the exact task in the first sentence. Restate a piece of the question so it is unmistakable you are on target.
  2. Cite a specific piece of evidence: a name, place, policy, event, or date. Specific beats vague every time.
  3. Explain (for parts that say “explain” only): connect your evidence to the task with how or why. One good causal sentence usually seals the point.
Read the verb in each part

Parts a and b usually say identify or describe, which are quick. Part c almost always says explain, and that is the point students lose the most. “Explain” means show the mechanism, not just name a fact.

Class rules that win SAQ pointsWrite in past tense, in complete sentences, and label each answer (a) (b) (c). Never quote or simply reword the stimulus: add something new (an example, an analysis, an interpretation). There is no partial credit, so make each part unmistakably complete. Before you write, check three things in the prompt: the time period, the geographic limit (Eurasia, the Americas, and so on), and the SPICE-T theme it targets.
Worked example: a Part C that earns Task: “Explain one reason why demand for enslaved labor increased in the Americas, c. 1450–1750.”

“The spread of labor-intensive sugar plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean created enormous demand for workers, and because Native populations had collapsed from disease, planters turned to enslaved Africans to keep the plantations running.” Why it earns: it names specific evidence (sugar plantations, disease collapse) and explains the mechanism with “because.” A reader who never saw the source could follow the cause and effect.
Wins the point
  • Answers the exact task asked
  • Uses a specific, correct example
  • Part C shows how/why, not just what
  • Labeled (a)(b)(c), complete sentences
Loses the point
  • Writes a thesis or intro (wastes time)
  • Vague: “people were unhappy”
  • Restates the source instead of explaining
  • Answers a nearby fact, not the task
Time disciplineBudget about 12 to 13 minutes per SAQ. If a part is not coming to you, write your best specific sentence and move on. A partial, specific answer beats a perfect answer you never reach on the next question.
Task Two
04.  The LEQ: Long Essay Question
Your Argument, Your Evidence

Six points, one argument

6 points
~40 min
1 required prompt
No documents

On the 2027 exam you get one required prompt (no more choosing among three) plus a brief introductory statement that orients you to the task. You write a full essay from your own knowledge, since there are no documents to lean on. You are not expected to cover the entire time period: pick the one or two examples you know best and build your argument there.

The 6-point rubric: what each point requires
  • 1 pt
    Thesis / Claim
    A historically defensible claim that responds to the prompt and establishes a line of reasoning. One or two sentences in the intro or conclusion. Must be more than a restatement of the prompt.
  • 1 pt
    Contextualization  ★
    Describe a broader historical situation (events before or around the topic) that is relevant to the prompt. Must be more than a phrase, usually a few sentences. This is a free, learnable point that too many students throw away. See the full breakdown below.
  • 1 pt
    Evidence: PROVIDE two examples
    Provide at least two specific, relevant historical examples (names, events, policies). This is the low bar: naming accurate evidence on the topic earns it. Aim for 3 to 5 so one weak example does not sink you.
  • +1 pt
    Evidence: SUPPORT the argument
    Use those examples to support your argument, not merely list them. Each piece must do a job for your thesis. Evidence sitting in your context paragraph does not count here, so re-use it in the body.
  • 1 pt
    Historical reasoning
    Use comparison, causation, or continuity & change to frame and structure the whole response, not as a stray transition word.
  • 1 pt
    Complexity
    Show a complex understanding (nuance, a qualification, multiple causes/effects, or an insightful connection) developed with evidence and sustained across the essay. The hardest point on the exam.
Point worth harping on ★ Contextualization
Set the stage before you make your case.

Contextualization is one paragraph, usually at the start of your essay, right before your thesis, where you zoom out and describe the bigger historical situation your topic sits inside. Think of it the way your class does: it is the opening crawl of Star Wars, the prologue of Romeo and Juliet, or the “previously on…” recap before a new episode. It gives the reader the background they need before the story starts. It is one of the easiest points to earn and one of the most commonly lost, because students give a single phrase instead of a real, developed description.

What earns the point

You must describe a relevant broader development (before, during, or just after the period) in more than a phrase. Aim for 2 to 4 full sentences. Start broad and narrow down to the prompt, stay within about 100 years of your topic, and remember you cannot earn evidence points inside your context, so save your specific proof for the body.

A reliable formula
Before / around [the period], [describe a broad, relevant development in a couple of sentences]. This set the stage for [the prompt's topic] because [one sentence linking the background to what you will argue].
Worked example: LEQ on the Atlantic Revolutions “In the century before the Atlantic Revolutions, Enlightenment thinkers such as Locke and Rousseau spread the idea that government should rest on the consent of the governed rather than the divine right of kings. These ideas circulated through books, salons, and pamphlets across Europe and its colonies, and they gave educated elites a new language for questioning absolute rule. This set the stage for the revolutions of the period, because reformers now had a framework to justify overthrowing monarchs.” Why it earns: it describes a real, relevant development across several sentences, and the last line steers it into the argument. It is background, not the argument itself.
Where students throw it away

A passing phrase (“During the Enlightenment…”) is not enough. A name-drop list with no development does not count. Context that never connects to the prompt does not count. And do not put your thesis or specific evidence in this slot: context is the wide backdrop, not your argument.

Build it in this order
  1. Deconstruct the prompt (1 min). Read the intro statement, then circle the command verb, the topic, any region, and the time frame. Watch for “and/or”: “social and/or cultural” means you may write about either or both.
  2. Brainstorm and prewrite (5 min). Sketch a quick Venn diagram (comparison), T-chart (continuity & change), or fishbone (causation), and list every specific fact you know before you write. Do not skip this.
  3. Write contextualization first (see the spotlight above): a few sentences of the bigger picture that lead into your thesis.
  4. Write the thesis: a claim with a qualifier and a line of reasoning (see the formula below).
  5. Write 2 to 3 body paragraphs, each organized by your reasoning skill, each using specific evidence to prove a piece of the claim.
  6. Land the complexity: weave in a counter-case, a limit, or a comparison and explain why it matters. Not a tacked-on last sentence.
How to build the thesis
Evaluative adverb + “such as” or “as seen through.”

A thesis that earns the point does two jobs at once: it takes a position and it previews the specific evidence that will prove it. You do both with two small tools.

1. An evaluative adverb sets your position

Answer the prompt and stake a degree of judgment with an adverb: significantly, largely, primarily, greatly, drastically, to a great extent, to a limited extent. The adverb is what turns a flat restatement of the prompt into a defensible claim.

2. “Such as” or “as seen through” previews your evidence

Right after the claim, use “such as” or “as seen through” to name the two or three specific examples or categories you will argue. Those become your body paragraphs, so this one move also builds your outline.

[Development] [evaluative adverb] [answer to the prompt], as seen through [specific evidence 1] and [specific evidence 2].
Worked example: “as seen through” “Mongol expansion greatly transformed Eurasia, as seen through the revival of Silk Road trade under the Pax Mongolica and the wide exchange of technologies like gunpowder and paper money.” The adverb (“greatly”) stakes a position; “as seen through” names the two specific pieces of evidence that become the two body paragraphs.
Worked example: “such as” “Nineteenth-century reform movements brought about significant social and political change, such as the creation of labor unions and the passage of laws ending child labor.” Same recipe: an adverb for the degree of judgment, then “such as” to preview the specific evidence you will develop.
Add nuance with an “Although…” opener

Although [the other side], [your position] to a [great/limited] extent, as seen through [evidence 1] and [evidence 2]. Opening with “although” folds in a counter-case, which can also push you toward the complexity point. Example: “Although internal rebellions weakened the Qing, foreign involvement was primarily responsible for its collapse, as seen through the opium trade's drain on silver and the loss of sovereignty under the unequal treaties.”

Two class-tested thesis tricks1. Pretend the prompt is an SAQ: if you covered up the rest of your essay, your thesis alone should fully answer the question. 2. Use the language of the prompt. And if your opening thesis came out clumsy, rewrite a sharper version in your conclusion; the reader will credit the better one.
The most common LEQ missesEvidence that just sits there (named but never tied to the claim) and a reasoning skill that never structures anything (one “because” buried in paragraph three). Make your reasoning the skeleton of the whole essay, and after every fact ask “how does this prove my thesis?”
Task Three
05.  The DBQ: Document-Based Question
The Big One: 25% of the Exam

Seven documents, seven points

7 points
~60 min
7 documents
15-min reading period

The DBQ is the LEQ plus a stack of evidence and a sourcing skill. You get seven documents (text and images) and you build an argument that uses them and your own outside knowledge. It is the single most valuable essay on the exam, so it is worth a deliberate routine.

Use the 15-minute reading period like a pro
  1. Read the prompt first and decide a rough position before you touch a document.
  2. Annotate each document as you read. Write one margin note for its main idea, and note the author, their situation, and their purpose from the source line (you will need this for sourcing).
  3. Group the documents into 2 or 3 buckets that will become your body paragraphs (for and against, or by reasoning category). Plan to use at least 6 of the 7.
  4. Jot outside evidence in the margin: a fact you know that is not in any document. You need at least one, it must be different from your contextualization, and it should stay in the same region and period as the documents.
The 7-point rubric: where the extra points live
  • 1 pt
    Thesis / Claim
    Same as the LEQ: a defensible claim with a line of reasoning that takes a position on the extent.
  • 1 pt
    Contextualization  ★
    A broader historical situation relevant to the prompt, described in more than a phrase, before you dive into the documents. Same skill as the LEQ, and just as free. See the full breakdown below.
  • 1 pt
    Evidence from the documents: USE 3
    Accurately describe the content (not just quote) of at least three documents relevant to the prompt. The low bar: it shows you understood them.
  • +1 pt
    Documents SUPPORT the argument: use 4
    Use the content of at least four documents to support your argument, not just describe them. Strategy: actually use 6 or 7, which also helps earn complexity.
  • 1 pt
    Evidence beyond the documents
    Use at least one specific piece of outside knowledge, relevant to the argument and different from your contextualization. Keep it in the same region and time period as the documents.
  • 1 pt
    Sourcing / HIPP: source 2  ★
    For at least two documents, explain how or why the source's point of view, purpose, historical situation, or audience is relevant to your argument. Explain why it matters, do not just name it. Strategy: source 4, which helps earn complexity. See the full breakdown below.
  • 1 pt
    Complexity
    A sophisticated argument, sustained across the essay. Common ways in: use all 7 documents to support the argument, source 4 documents, explain multiple perspectives or both cause and effect, or connect across regions and periods.
Point worth harping on ★ Contextualization
Same skill as the LEQ. Do not skip it here either.

Contextualization on the DBQ works exactly like the LEQ version: a developed paragraph of broader background (2 to 4 sentences) placed before you start using documents. The trap unique to the DBQ is confusing three different things, so keep them straight:

Contextualization is the wide backdrop you supply from your own knowledge. Outside evidence is one specific fact inside your argument that is not in the documents (and must be different from your context). Document evidence comes from the seven sources. Three separate points, so use three different pieces of information.

A reliable formula
In the [decades] surrounding this topic, [describe a broad, relevant trend in a couple of sentences]. This wider situation is the backdrop against which [the documents / the events in the prompt] should be understood.
Worked example: DBQ on the collapse of the Qing “By the second half of the 1800s, industrialized Western powers were using military force and economic pressure to extract concessions from weaker states across Asia, from the Ottoman Empire to Qing China. Gunboats, unequal treaties, and demands for open trade steadily eroded these states' control over their own affairs. This wider wave of imperialism is the backdrop against which the Qing dynasty's mounting troubles must be understood.” Why it earns: several sentences describing a genuinely broader development, clearly tied to the prompt's topic and era, and it does not reuse a document or the outside-evidence fact.
Where students throw it away

A single phrase, a bare name-drop, background that never connects to the prompt, or reusing the same fact for context and for outside evidence (which costs you the second point). Context is broad and general; outside evidence is one narrow, specific fact.

Sourcing done right: the H.I.P.P. move

Sourcing is the DBQ's signature skill and the point most students fumble. It is really one good sentence attached to a document: you name a feature of the source (not its content), explain how that feature shapes what the document says, and connect it to your argument. You must do this accurately for at least two documents to earn the point, but aim for four: it is a safety net if you slip on one, and sourcing four helps earn the complexity point.

H

Historical situation

What was happening in the world when and where this was made? Ask: how does that moment shape the message?

I

Intended audience

Who was the author speaking or writing to? Ask: how would the author shape the message for those specific readers?

P

Purpose

Why did the author create this? Ask: what were they trying to make happen, and how does that goal color what they include?

P

Point of view

Who is the author (their job, class, nationality, religion, group)? Ask: how does that position bias what they emphasize or leave out?

The 3-move sourcing sentence1. Identify a specific HIPP feature, pulling detail from the source line above the document. 2. Explain with a “which means…” or “therefore…” bridge: how does that feature shape what the source stresses, includes, or omits? 3. Connect it to your argument about the prompt. Miss any of the three and the point does not count.
What to actually look for in the source lineThe italic source line above each document is where sourcing lives. For point of view, note the author's role, nationality, religion, class, or occupation. For purpose and audience, note the type of document: a letter to an official, a government decree, a newspaper article, a private diary, a political poster, a work of art. For reliability, compare the date it was made to when the events happened: a memoir written decades later is shaped by hindsight.
Where the sourcing sentence goesDo not source in a separate paragraph. When you use a document as evidence, add the sourcing sentence right after it: give the evidence, then explain the HIPP feature and why it shapes what the document does or does not say.
Worked example A: sourcing by Point of View “Because this document is a revolutionary proclamation written by young Chinese students studying abroad (point of view), its authors were committed opponents of Manchu rule, which means it deliberately frames the dynasty's fall as an internal, patriotic uprising and downplays foreign pressure. This supports my argument that the collapse was driven as much from within as from outside.” Why it earns: it names the POV using detail from the source line, explains how that shapes the content with “which means,” and ties it to the argument. Three moves, one point.
Worked example B: sourcing by Purpose “This political cartoon was published in a Shanghai newspaper to warn the Chinese public that Western powers were carving up their country (purpose), therefore it exaggerates China's helplessness to stir alarm and national feeling, which strengthens the case that fear of foreign domination was fueling calls for change.” Why it earns: purpose is named and, crucially, the sentence explains how that purpose bends the message, then links it to the argument.
Accurate sourcing
  • Names a feature of the source, not its content
  • Uses detail from the source line (who, when, why)
  • Explains how that feature shapes the document
  • Connects the point to your argument
  • Done for 3 different documents
Sourcing that earns nothing
  • Just naming it: “The audience is the government.”
  • “The author is biased” with no how or why
  • Summarizing what the document says
  • Sourcing only 1 or 2 documents
  • No link back to the argument
Fast way to remember itContent answers “what does the document say?” Sourcing answers “why should I trust it, and what is it shaped by?” If your sentence could be written by someone who only read the source line and never read the document body, you are sourcing. If it retells the document, you are not.
The classic DBQ trapSummarizing the documents in order (“Document 1 says… Document 2 says…”). That is a book report, not an argument. Instead, group documents by what they prove, cite two or three together in a single sentence to back one claim, and keep your thesis driving every paragraph.
Test Day
06.  The game plan and the questions everyone asks

Section II gives you 100 minutes for the DBQ and LEQ together, and the proctor will suggest a split. Here is the plan that keeps you from running out of time on the essay worth the most.

BlockTaskHow to spend it
0–15 minDBQ readingRead prompt, annotate all 7 docs, group them, jot outside evidence and a thesis.
15–60 minDBQ writingAbout 45 min to write. Context, then thesis, then grouped body paragraphs with sourcing, then complexity.
60–100 minLEQAbout 40 min. Read the prompt and its intro statement, prewrite for 5, write for about 33, leave 2 to sharpen the thesis in your conclusion.
No, but it helps you and the reader. A reliable structure is a context paragraph, then thesis, then body paragraphs, then complexity woven through and reinforced at the end. Putting each point where the reader expects it means they never have to hunt, and a two-minute grader who cannot find your point cannot award it.
Length is not scored; points are. That said, a full DBQ or LEQ that earns most of its points is usually four to six solid paragraphs. Do not pad. A tight essay that nails every rubric line beats a long one that wanders. Spend your words on specific evidence, not adjectives.
Get specific. The gap between a mid essay and a high one is almost always concrete evidence (real names, dates, policies, and places) used to prove a claim, instead of vague generalizations. “Trade increased” earns little; “the Mongol yam relay system secured the Silk Roads, reviving caravan trade between China and Persia” earns a lot.
Complexity is the least-earned point on the whole exam, and chasing it can wreck your timing. Lock in the reliable points first (thesis, context, evidence, sourcing). Then reach for complexity by qualifying your own argument with real evidence (“although…” or a cross-region comparison) rather than a hollow “it was complicated” sentence.
